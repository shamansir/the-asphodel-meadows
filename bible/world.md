# Asphodel Meadows

## The world — Season 1: *The Great Hiatus*

The fixed rules of the universe. The writer agent reads this every chunk. Nothing here
changes without a human editing this file.

---

## Premise

On 4 May 1891 Sherlock Holmes went over the Reichenbach Falls locked in the arms of
Professor Moriarty. He did not return to London until 1894.

Doyle never adequately explained those three years. Holmes's own account — Tibet, Persia,
Khartoum — is thin, unverifiable, and told to a man too relieved to interrogate it. That
hole in the canon is where this season lives.

He did not climb out of the falls. He went down.

Hermes met him at the bottom, as Hermes meets everyone, and conducted him to the house of
Hades. There the paperwork failed: Holmes is not dead. He is not alive either. Nobody in
the administration can produce the correct form, and until they can, he is a guest.

A guest with nothing to do, in a house full of immortals who have been lying to each other
for four thousand years.

The Lord of the Dead, having no precedent and a great deal of unexplained trouble, retains
him. Consulting detective, sole practitioner, terms unspecified.

## The tonal contract

*Knives Out*, not *Clash of the Titans*.

The Olympians are a rich, petty, mutually-loathing family with too much money, too much
time, and no self-awareness whatsoever. Holmes is the outsider loose in their house.

**The one rule that keeps this from going stupid: play the deduction completely straight.**
The comedy sits on top of real detective work; it never replaces it. Every chain of
inference must hold up on a re-read, even when the case is a stolen sandwich. If the
solution only works because it is funny, it is wrong and the chunk fails.

Nobody in this world knows they are in a comedy. Everyone is entirely serious about their
own concerns. That is where the jokes come from.

---

## Death, and why there are no murders

Nobody here can die. They are already dead, or they were never mortal.

This is a constraint, not a limitation. It forces better cases than murder would:

- theft, especially of things that should be impossible to steal
- a soul filed under the wrong name, or under no name
- something let out
- a prophecy altered after issue
- adulterated ambrosia
- a debt paid twice
- someone remembering an event that has not happened

**The victim is usually still present**, wandering into the investigation to complain about
its progress, contradicting their own earlier statement, and taking it personally.

Consequences in this world are administrative, social, or eternal — never fatal. A guilty
verdict means a reassignment, a humiliation, or a very long time spent doing something
tedious. Those stakes are real to the people here, and the writer must treat them as real.

## Geography

**The House of Hades.** Not a torture pit. A vast, cold, badly-lit civil service that has
been running without reform since before Athens. Filing halls, waiting rooms, the Asphodel
concourse, a canteen. The horror is procedural.

**The Bank.** Where Charon works, where the newly dead queue, and where most witnesses can
be found because everyone passes through eventually.

**The Asphodel Meadows.** The ordinary dead go here — a grey, pleasant, absolutely endless
park. It is the largest thing in this world by a wide margin, which is why the world is
named for it: not for the palace, not for the punishments, but for the enormous grey middle
where almost everyone ends up. Elysium is gated and everyone resents it.

**The Boulder.** Sisyphus's hill, visible from most of the upper house. A permanent
landmark and a permanent witness.

**221B, London, 1891.** Seen rarely and only at a distance — through Mycroft's letters,
through scrying, through Holmes's memory. It is always slightly wrong when it appears,
because it is remembered rather than observed.

## Time

Playback runs at 1:1 with the viewer's wall clock. That is the shared-moment promise and it
does not bend.

Story time is a different matter, and the underworld solves it: **there is no calendar
down there.** Nothing has a date. Time is stated only in relation to Persephone's terms —
"since she came down", "before she goes up" — and that is the only clock the world admits.

So the season is not three real years long. It is however many of her cycles the arcs need.
When she leaves, the house changes; when she returns, it changes back. Her descent and
ascent are the season's structural beats, and the writer should use them.

London advances only when a letter arrives.

---

## The mortal link

Canon: Mycroft was the only living person who knew Holmes survived, and he funded the
Hiatus. That stays true here, and it is the world's single channel to the living.

Hermes carries the letters — Diogenes Club to the House and back. He reads them. He is
not sorry.

This means: Holmes can learn about London, slowly and unreliably. London cannot learn about
him. Watson believes his friend is dead, and is grieving in a city Holmes can watch and
cannot reach. Do not spend this cheaply; it is the season's most expensive card.

---

## Rules the writer may not break

1. **Nobody dies.** No exceptions, no near-misses played for tension.
2. **No god may simply solve it.** Divine power is enormous and specifically useless for
   knowing who did something. Establish why the obvious magical shortcut fails, once, and
   never revisit it.
3. **Holmes never uses magic.** He observes, he infers, he is occasionally wrong. His
   entire value here is that he is the only one in the building who cannot cheat.
4. **The solution must be visible to the viewer first.** Every clue Holmes uses gets an
   `insert` shot before he uses it. No off-screen deductions.
5. **Holmes is not omniscient.** He is wrong at least once per case, visibly, and the wrong
   inference must be as well-constructed as the right one.
6. **No prophecy that resolves a case.** Prophecy is a source of cases, never a solution.
7. **Nobody explains the crossover.** No character remarks that a Victorian detective and
   the Greek pantheon are an odd combination. It is not odd to them.

## Case structure

One case runs across several chunks. A chunk is a day, so a case is a few days.

```
the complaint      someone arrives aggrieved; the facts are wrong from the start
the walk-through   Holmes examines; inserts on every clue; Hermes narrates badly
the wrong idea     a confident, well-built, incorrect conclusion
the interference   a god makes it worse for reasons of their own
the reversal       the clue that was on screen in act one
the accusation     staged, theatrical, in a room containing everyone
the aftermath      the guilty party is not punished so much as inconvenienced
```

Between cases, run domestic chunks — the house, the queue, the canteen, the grievances.
Those are where the memory system does its work, and where the characters become people
rather than functions.

### Case titles

Administrative or ferry register, from inside the world. Never a pun on the premise, for
the same reason the show has no title card joke: nobody here finds any of this remarkable.

Good: *The Second Obol*. *Nine Hundred and Four*. *Unclaimed Effects*. *No Change Given*.
Bad: anything with "elementary", "deduction", or a Baker Street reference.

Reserved, to be used when the case earns it:

- **Pending** — Holmes's own file status: not alive, not dead, no form exists. Save it for
  the case that is actually about *him* rather than about someone else's trouble. It is the
  best title we have and it should only be spent once.

## The season arc

Moriarty went over the falls too.

He is not in the filing system. Nobody has processed him, nobody has seen him, and the
administration would very much like this not to be discussed. Holmes does not know yet.

Seed it in the background — a missing form, a locked corridor, a name Charon will not
say — and let the cases accumulate evidence the audience assembles before Holmes does.

The season ends when Holmes learns what happened to Moriarty and, as a consequence,
returns to London in 1894. That is the finale (§6), and it is fixed. Everything before it
is open.

---

## Cast discipline

Six characters have live memory files. Five speaking parts per scene is the ceiling, and
three is better. Recurring characters may appear without being in the prompt's cast list.

Dormant characters — Watson, Mycroft, Mrs Hudson, Moriarty — each have a file stating the
conditions under which they may enter. The writer agent may not introduce them on its own;
a human moves a file from `dormant` to `active`.
